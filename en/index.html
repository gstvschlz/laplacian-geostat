What is the Laplacian?

an interactive explainer · Walker Lake data

One local rule connects spatial curvature, kriging estimation, variogram range, and conditional simulation: compare each cell to the average of its neighbours.

1 operator 2 estimation 3 range κ 4 on a map 5 simulation 6 conditional
step 1 of 6

The operator: value minus neighbour average

The discrete Laplacian measures how much a cell sticks out from its immediate neighbours. On a flat profile or a straight slope, every interior cell equals the average of its left and right neighbours, so its Laplacian is 0. On a peak it is positive and its bar points up in solid ink; in a pit it is negative and its bar points down in grey.

Points: dark = pit (−), light = peak (+). Bars: solid up = peak, grey down = pit. Drag any point up or down
step 2 of 6

Estimation: hold the data, make the Laplacian zero

Suppose you only know the value at a few sample locations. If you hold those data points fixed and repeatedly replace every other cell by the average of its two neighbours, the Laplacian decays to zero everywhere else. The profile settles into straight lines between the samples—kriging with a linear variogram.

Click or drag on the upper plot to place or move held data points. Double-click or Shift-click a held point to remove it.

step 3 of 6

Adding κ: sagging toward the mean

Instead of dividing the sum of the two neighbours by 2, divide by 2 + κ². Now each step multiplies the neighbour average by a fraction slightly below one, pulling the estimate toward the background mean (zero) away from the data. The parameter κ sets the correlation range: this is simple kriging with an exponential variogram.

Drag the κ slider to watch the estimate sag from straight lines (grey) toward the mean. Click or drag on the plot to move or add samples.

step 5 of 6

Simulation: feeding the operator with white noise

Kriging is smoother than reality because it zeroes the Laplacian away from data. What if we instead set each cell's Laplacian to a random number drawn from white noise? Solving (κ² I + L) z = noise by the exact same neighbour relaxation (adding each cell's fixed random number to its 4-neighbour sum before dividing by 4 + κ²) filters white noise into a spatial field with a Matérn variogram.

step 6 of 6

Both at once: conditional simulation

Hold the Walker Lake sample blocks at their measured values and feed white noise into the free cells. Every realization honours the hard data and reproduces the spatial texture away from the samples. Toggle the noise off and the field collapses back to the smooth kriging estimate.

The model behind all six steps is a Gaussian Markov random field with precision (inverse covariance) Q = τ(κ² I + L)^α, where L is the graph Laplacian, κ sets the inverse range, α sets the smoothness, and τ scales the variance (Lindgren, Rue & Lindström, 2011).
